# Your positioning for this interview

Last time the story was "I build and own big products". **That is not the story here, and you have deliberately changed your CV to match.** The new story is:

> *A computer science student who builds web applications and, more importantly, has actually put them on Linux servers and kept them running - DNS, Nginx, HTTPS, ports, logs. I know the network from the software side, I know I have a lot to learn about the hardware side, and I learn quickly when someone teaches me.*

Three things that story does for you:

1. **It is honest.** Every part of it is on your CV and you can defend all of it.
2. **It is humble.** You are not claiming to be a network engineer. You are claiming to be teachable, which is exactly what a first-line support team wants.
3. **It bridges.** Every deployment thing you have done - a reverse proxy, a DNS A record, a TLS certificate, a firewall port - is a real networking concept. You are not a stranger to their world; you have met it from the other side.

## The register rule - important

You are not going to talk about "ownership" tonight or tomorrow. Not once. Your CV now says **Full Stack Developer Intern**, so any "I owned the product" language would contradict the paper in front of them and make you look inflated.

Use this vocabulary instead:

| Don't say | Say |
|---|---|
| "I owned the whole product" | "I worked on it and I got to see the whole thing end to end" |
| "I architected a scalable system" | "I built it, and here is how it works" |
| "I led the development" | "I was the developer on it, and I asked a lot of questions" |
| "I'm an expert in X" | "I've used X a fair amount - here's what I understand about it" |

The humility is not a performance. It is genuinely the right register for a first-line role in a very large company, and interviewers detect the difference instantly.

## The one rule about your projects

Your projects are bigger and more complicated than what is now written on your CV. That is intentional. **Describe them at the size they are written.** Simple description, simple follow-up questions, complete confident answers.

But **never lie about a technical fact.** Simplified is fine. Wrong is fatal - especially in round 2, where they will dig. Document 03 gives you the exact wording for each project, plus the deeper layer to reveal *only if* you are asked directly.

# Three habits for the room

1. **Listen to the whole question before answering.** The JD literally lists "be an active listener" as a required skill. If a question is long, it is fine to say "just so I understand - are you asking about X or about Y?" That scores points; it does not lose them.
2. **If you don't know, say so, then reason out loud.** "I haven't worked with that directly. Can I tell you what I do know that's related, and what I'd guess?" That is a good answer. Bluffing is the only bad answer.
3. **Short answer first, then stop.** Two or three sentences, then let them ask. Do not empty the whole document into one answer.

# Say the name right

It is **BT** or **BT Group** - two letters, "B-T". In the UK it grew out of **British Telecom**, and people do say "British Telecom" casually, but the company is BT Group and has been since 2001. Say "BT Group" and you sound current.
